## Reflection

When I saw that NHS England had published four cyber alerts on the same day, it immediately caught my attention. Having worked in the NHS during the WannaCry attack, I know how important it is to take vulnerabilities seriously and act quickly when security updates are released.

WannaCry was a real eye-opener for many people. At the time, cyber security suddenly became everyone's concern, not just the IT department's. It highlighted how vulnerable organisations can be when systems aren't patched and how quickly a technical issue can affect day-to-day operations.

Reading these alerts, I found myself thinking back to those lessons. Although the alerts don't necessarily mean there are more cyber threats than before, they do show just how many vulnerabilities security teams have to deal with on a regular basis. Four significant alerts in a single day demonstrates the pace at which new vulnerabilities are being discovered and the pressure organisations are under to keep systems secure.

What really stood out to me was that the vulnerabilities affected technologies that are widely used across organisations, including PHP, VMware vCenter, NGINX and F5 systems. These aren't niche products. They're technologies that many organisations rely on every day to deliver critical services. If vulnerabilities in these systems aren't addressed quickly, the impact could be significant.

This linked directly to what we've been covering recently on the Level 3 Cyber Security course. We've spent time looking at vulnerabilities, patching, updates and the importance of having a structured response when weaknesses are identified. Seeing real-world examples of these issues being reported by NHS England helped reinforce why those topics matter. It's one thing to learn about patch management in theory, but it's another to see multiple critical vulnerabilities being actively tracked and communicated to organisations.

The alerts also reminded me that patching isn't always the most exciting part of cyber security, but it's often one of the most important. Looking back at WannaCry, one of the biggest lessons learned across the NHS was the importance of maintaining systems and applying updates in a timely manner. These latest alerts show that the same principle still applies today.

I also have a lot of respect for the work carried out by NHS England's cyber teams. Seeing four alerts released on the same day made me think about the amount of monitoring, assessment and communication that must happen behind the scenes. Cyber threats don't stop, and neither can the organisations responsible for protecting critical services.

Overall, these alerts reinforced the importance of vulnerability management, patching and staying informed about emerging threats. They also reminded me why cyber security matters in the first place. It's not just about protecting systems or data. It's about protecting the services that people depend on every day. Having witnessed the impact of WannaCry and now studying cyber security in more depth, I can see how lessons learned from previous incidents continue to shape the way organisations respond to vulnerabilities and build resilience against future threats.
