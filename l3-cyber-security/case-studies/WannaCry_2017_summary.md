# Case Study: WannaCry and the NHS

## Introduction

The WannaCry attack against the NHS in May 2017 remains one of the most significant cyber security incidents to affect public services in the UK. It demonstrated how a known vulnerability, combined with poor patch management and legacy systems, could have a major impact on a critical national service.

What makes this incident particularly interesting is that the vulnerability being exploited had already been identified, and a security patch was available before the attack occurred. In many ways, the incident was not caused by a lack of technology but by a failure to consistently implement basic cyber security controls.

---

## What Happened?

On 12 May 2017, the WannaCry ransomware attack spread rapidly around the world. The malware encrypted files on infected computers and displayed a ransom note demanding payment in Bitcoin.

The NHS was heavily affected. At least 81 NHS trusts experienced disruption and hundreds of other healthcare organisations, including GP practices, were impacted. Thousands of appointments and operations were cancelled, and staff were forced to revert to paper-based processes to continue delivering patient care.

Importantly, there was no evidence that patient data was stolen. The main impact was on the availability of systems rather than the confidentiality of information.

---

## How Did WannaCry Work?

WannaCry was particularly dangerous because it combined two different forms of attack.

### Ransomware

- Encrypted files on infected systems.
- Displayed a ransom demand requiring payment in Bitcoin.
- Prevented users from accessing critical data and applications.

### Worm Functionality

- Enabled the malware to spread automatically
