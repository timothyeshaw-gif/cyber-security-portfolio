# 🖥️ Microsoft Network Intrusion Attempt – Computer Misuse Act Case Study

## 📌 Overview

This case study examines an attempted attack against Microsoft and analyses how the incident relates to the Computer Misuse Act 1990 (CMA).

According to reports, two individuals were arrested after allegedly attempting to gain access to Microsoft's internal systems in order to steal customer data.

The case provides a useful example of how cyber security legislation can be applied to a real-world incident.

---

## ⚖️ Relevant Legislation

The primary legislation applicable to this incident is:

- Computer Misuse Act 1990

The Act makes unauthorised access to computer systems and data a criminal offence.

---

## 🧩 Which Computer Misuse Act Offences May Have Been Committed?

### Section 1 – Unauthorised Access to Computer Material

Section 1 makes it an offence to access, or attempt to access, computer systems without permission.

In this case:

- The suspects allegedly attempted to access Microsoft's internal systems.
- The access was not authorised.
- Therefore, the activity may fall under Section 1 of the Computer Misuse Act 1990.

---

### Section 2 – Unauthorised Access with Intent to Commit Further Offences

Section 2 applies when unauthorised access is carried out with the intention of committing another offence.

In this case:

- The suspects were reportedly attempting to obtain customer information.
- Stolen customer data could potentially be used for fraud, identity theft, or other criminal activity.
- Therefore, the activity may also fall under Section 2 of the Computer Misuse Act 1990.

---

### Section 3 – Unauthorised Acts with Intent to Impair Operation of a Computer

Section 3 applies when an individual performs unauthorised acts that impair, or are intended to impair:

- The operation of a computer system.
- Access to programs or data.
- The reliability or availability of systems.

Examples include:

- Malware attacks
- Ransomware deployment
- Denial-of-Service attacks
- System disruption
- Data corruption

Based on the information available, there is no evidence that the suspects caused damage or disruption to Microsoft's systems.

However, if the attack had:

- Damaged systems,
- Corrupted data,
- Reduced service availability,
- Installed malicious software, or
- Disrupted Microsoft's operations,

then Section 3 of the Computer Misuse Act 1990 could also have been relevant.

---

## ✅ Conclusion

Based on the information reported, the strongest legal connection is to:

- Section 1 – Unauthorised Access to Computer Material
- Section 2 – Unauthorised Access with Intent to Commit Further Offences

Section 3 was not reported as part of the case. However, it could potentially have applied if evidence showed that Microsoft's systems were impaired, damaged, or disrupted by the intrusion attempt.

## 🎯 Why Would Attackers Target Microsoft?

The BBC article stated that the suspects were:

> "seeking to steal customer data from the software giant."

Possible motivations include:

### Financial Gain

Customer information may be valuable to cyber criminals and could potentially be used for fraud or sold to other criminals.

### High-Value Target

Microsoft is one of the world's largest technology companies and stores significant amounts of valuable information.

### Criminal Exploitation

Access to customer information may enable:

- Identity theft
- Credential theft
- Fraud
- Account compromise
- Sale of information on criminal marketplaces

---

## 🛡️ How Similar Attacks Can Be Prevented

Organisations can reduce the likelihood of similar attacks by implementing strong cyber security controls.

### Security Monitoring

- Continuous monitoring of systems and networks.
- Detection of suspicious behaviour and intrusion attempts.

### Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)

- Require MFA for all sensitive systems.
- Reduce risks associated with stolen credentials.

### Least Privilege Access

- Restrict access to sensitive systems and information.
- Ensure staff only have the permissions necessary for their role.

### Penetration Testing

- Conduct regular security assessments.
- Identify vulnerabilities before attackers can exploit them.

### Security Awareness Training

- Train staff to recognise phishing and social engineering attacks.
- Improve organisational security culture.

### International Cooperation

- Improve collaboration between law enforcement agencies.
- Support cross-border investigations involving cybercrime.
