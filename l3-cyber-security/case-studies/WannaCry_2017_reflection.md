## Portfolio Reflection

One reason I chose to explore the WannaCry incident in detail is that I was working within NHS commissioning support services in East London when the attack occurred. Although I was not involved in the technical response, I remember the uncertainty across the NHS as organisations tried to understand the scale of the incident and minimise disruption to patient services.

Looking back with the benefit of nearly a decade of hindsight and my subsequent studies in cyber security, what strikes me most is not how sophisticated the attack was, but how preventable much of the disruption appears to have been.

Before researching the incident, I assumed that major cyber attacks were typically the result of highly advanced techniques that organisations could do little to defend against. However, the more I learned about WannaCry, the more I realised that its success was rooted in the failure to consistently apply fundamental security controls. Microsoft had already released a patch for the vulnerability being exploited, warnings had been issued, and the risks were understood. Nevertheless, many systems remained exposed.

The incident reinforced the importance of patch management, vulnerability management, asset visibility and business continuity planning. These areas may not attract the same attention as emerging threats or advanced detection technologies, but WannaCry demonstrates that weaknesses in basic controls can have significant operational consequences.

I was also particularly interested in the role played by Marcus Hutchins, whose discovery of the malware's kill switch dramatically reduced the spread of the attack. Whilst this undoubtedly limited the overall impact, it also highlighted an uncomfortable reality: the NHS benefited from a degree of good fortune. Had the kill switch not existed, or had it not been identified quickly, the disruption could have been significantly greater.

Another lesson I took from this case study is that cyber security cannot be viewed solely as a technical issue. The attack exposed challenges relating to governance, oversight, accountability, and risk management, demonstrating that organisational decisions can be just as important as technical controls in determining resilience.

My experience working within NHS commissioning support services also helped me appreciate how dependent modern healthcare had already become on technology. Even where clinical systems were not directly affected, disruption to communications, administration and supporting services could have a significant impact on the delivery of care. This reinforced my understanding that cyber security is ultimately about protecting organisational outcomes, not just systems and devices.

As someone moving into the cyber security profession, I consider WannaCry to be an important reminder that effective security is often about managing known risks well rather than relying on sophisticated technology to solve problems after they occur. The incident demonstrates how technical vulnerabilities, operational dependencies and governance weaknesses can combine to create a major service disruption.

Overall, this case study reinforced my belief that good cyber security is built upon strong fundamentals, consistent risk management and organisational resilience. Sometimes the biggest lessons come from attacks that should never have succeeded.
